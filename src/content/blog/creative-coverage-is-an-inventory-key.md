Creative discussions in most performance teams are about quality: which video is best, which headline converts, what to cut. Useful questions, all downstream of a more basic one that rarely gets asked.

**Can your campaign enter the auction at all?**

An automated app campaign assembles ads from the components you upload. Each surface it can serve on accepts particular shapes: a vertical video for a short-form feed, a landscape one for in-stream, a square image for a browse surface, text-only for a search result. If you have not uploaded a component that fits a surface, your campaign is not competing for that surface. Not competing badly — not competing.

This is why creative coverage behaves like an inventory key rather than a design preference, and why a campaign with excellent creative and thin coverage can be beaten by a campaign with mediocre creative and complete coverage.

## What the auction actually rewards

The mechanics make the point sharper. An ad's position in the auction is roughly:

> **bid × predicted click-through rate × predicted conversion rate**

Bid is one of three terms, and it is the only one you cannot improve without paying more. The other two are creative outcomes. A better-performing creative set raises the second and third terms, which means the same bid wins more auctions — you get cheaper inventory by being more relevant, not by paying more.

## Fill every slot before you optimise any of them

The sequencing here is not intuitive and it is where most teams get it backwards.

**First, hit the upload ceiling.** Typical limits run to five headlines, five long descriptions, twenty images, twenty videos and twenty HTML5 assets per ad group. Fill them. An asset that performs poorly overall can still be the one that wins a specific edge case — a particular surface, country or audience — and removing it removes that possibility. Until every slot is occupied, adding is strictly better than curating.

**Cover the shapes deliberately, not incidentally.** Images want square, portrait and landscape. Video wants landscape, vertical and square, at a spread of lengths from a few seconds to a minute. Every gap is a closed door. A vertical video in particular is what makes a campaign eligible for short-form feed inventory at all, and adding a single one is often the highest-return creative task available.

**Then, and only then, replace the weak ones.** After three to four weeks, assets have had a fair chance and their ratings mean something.

## Asset ratings are relative, and people misread them

This trips up almost everyone. Asset performance labels — learning, low, good, best — are **relative to the other assets in the same campaign**, not absolute measures of quality.

Three consequences follow that are worth stating plainly:

1. **Something is always rated low.** In any set, something is at the bottom. A "low" rating does not mean the asset is bad; it means it is the worst of the ones you uploaded. Replacing it and then replacing the new bottom, repeatedly, is a treadmill.
2. **Improving your best asset creates new low ratings.** Add a strong video and weaker assets get relabelled without changing at all.
3. **A campaign of uniformly excellent assets still has a "low" one.** Deleting it can make the set worse.

So: replace assets rated low, keep the good and the best, and never act on an asset still marked learning. Judging an asset before its rating settles is the same error as judging a campaign before its learning period ends.

## A refresh cadence that does not fight the system

- **Refresh every two to three months.** Frequently enough to counter fatigue, rarely enough for ratings to mean something.
- **Replace the weakest first**, not everything at once. Wholesale replacement resets the campaign's understanding of the entire set and you learn nothing about what actually worked.
- **Never remove an asset within two weeks of uploading it.** It has not finished being evaluated.
- **Add assets resembling your best ones.** The rating is a directional signal about what your audience responds to, and it is one of the few honest ones you get for free.

## Structure so the results are readable

Two habits make the reporting far more useful, and both cost nothing:

**One theme per ad group.** Start with at least three distinct themes — different use cases, different benefits, different framings — and give each its own ad group with full coverage. Mixed themes in one ad group produce a rating table that tells you which *asset* won and never which *idea* won, and the idea is the transferable finding.

**Encode metadata in asset filenames.** Length, theme, format, language, in the video title. Reporting will then group by those tags for free, and you can answer "do six-second cuts beat thirty-second ones for us" without a spreadsheet exercise. It takes seconds when uploading and it is unrecoverable later.

Then segment performance by network. Different surfaces reward different lengths and orientations, and the blended number hides that completely — an asset that looks average overall is often excellent on one surface and poor on another, which is a production instruction rather than a rating.

## The short version

Creative coverage decides which auctions you are allowed to enter. Creative quality decides how cheaply you win them. Most teams work on the second while the first is still half-finished — and filling the empty slots requires no new ideas at all, only the missing shapes.
