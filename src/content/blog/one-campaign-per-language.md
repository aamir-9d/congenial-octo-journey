Most app accounts are structured by geography. One campaign per country, or one per region, with creative in whatever language is dominant there.

It is the obvious structure and it quietly excludes a lot of people.

## The gap between a market and an audience

Take a single large market. The dominant language covers most of it, and that is what the campaign targets and what the creative is written in. But a market is not monolingual. Behind the dominant language sit substantial populations speaking others — in the tens of millions for the second language, and still in the millions several languages down.

A single country campaign with creative in one language competes for all of it and speaks properly to part of it. Those other-language users are technically in the targeting and effectively unaddressed: the ads reach them in a language they may not read comfortably, predicted click-through and conversion rates come out low against them, and the system quite reasonably stops showing them ads. Their absence never appears as a gap, because you cannot see the impressions you were never competitive for.

## Three structures, and when each applies

**Major language.** Location targeting set to the market, language targeting set to its dominant language, creative in that language. This is what most accounts already run, and there is nothing wrong with it — it is just incomplete.

**Long-tail language.** Same market, language targeting set to a secondary language spoken there, creative in *that* language. A separate campaign, not an extra ad group. This is where the unaddressed audience is.

**Global multilingual.** Location targeting set broadly or globally, language targeting set to one language, creative in that language. Speakers of a language are rarely confined to one country, and a diaspora is often a well-defined, underpriced audience that no geography-first structure will ever isolate.

## The part that makes it work, and the part that wastes the effort

Language targeting alone does nothing. If you split out a secondary-language campaign and run the same creative in the dominant language through it, you have added a campaign and changed nothing about what the user sees. Relevance did not move, so the predicted rates did not move, so the auction outcome does not move.

**The creative has to be in the language you are targeting.** That is the whole mechanism. Relevance raises predicted click-through and conversion rates, those raise your auction position at the same bid, and the result is cheaper inventory rather than more expensive reach.

Which also means the coverage discipline still applies inside each campaign — full asset slots, all the shapes, three or more themes per language. A localised campaign with two assets is not a fair test of localisation; it is a test of thin coverage.

## Cost, honestly

This is not free. Each additional language needs its own creative set, which means translation, voiceover or subtitles, and re-cut video. That is real production work, and it is why teams keep deferring it.

Two things make it cheaper than it looks. Machine dubbing and synthetic voiceover have become good enough for performance creative in many languages, so the marginal cost of a second language is now much closer to the cost of re-rendering than the cost of a reshoot. And you do not need parity — three themes in a second language is enough to learn whether the audience is there, which is a far smaller commitment than full localisation of everything.

Start with one secondary language in one market. If the economics work, the same argument applies to the next one, and you will have a real number to make it with.

## How to find where to start

**Count the population, not the countries.** For each market you already spend in, list the languages spoken and their approximate speaker numbers. The second language in a large market is frequently bigger than an entire market you are already running a dedicated campaign for.

**Look at where installs come from that you did not target.** Organic and spillover installs concentrated in a language group are a strong signal that demand exists ahead of the supply.

**Check the store listing first.** If the store page is not localised, a localised ad campaign sends people to a page they cannot read, and the install rate will make the language look unpromising when the ad was never the problem. Listing localisation is the cheaper half and should come first.

**Then run one campaign, properly resourced.** Full asset coverage, three themes, correct language, and enough budget and time to clear a learning period. A starved test will tell you nothing you can act on, and it will discourage the next one.

## The general point

Geography is a proxy for audience, and in most markets it is a poor one. Splitting by language is one of the few structural changes in performance marketing that adds reach without bidding more — because it does not buy more attention, it earns a better response from attention you were already paying for and wasting.
